01 How to listen to it

Each area gets four things, always in the same order: the wind (the direction it blows from, and its strength on the Beaufort scale), the sea state, the weather, and the visibility. So “Morrow: Westerly 5 to 7, veering northwesterly later. Rough. Showers. Good” means a fresh-to-near-gale west wind turning to the north-west in the second half of the day, waves of two and a half to four metres, showers, and clear air between them.

Veering, backing
The wind turning clockwise (south to west), or anticlockwise (west to south).
Soon, later
Six to twelve hours from when the forecast was issued, or more than twelve. Imminent is within six.
Gale 8
A mean wind of 34 to 40 knots, or gusts of 43 or more. Then severe gale 9, storm 10, violent storm 11, hurricane force 12.
Cyclonic
The wind will change direction a lot as a low passes over the area.
Slight, moderate, rough
Waves of half a metre to 1.25, up to 2.5, up to 4. Then very rough, high, very high, and phenomenal, which is more than fourteen metres.
Good, moderate, poor
You can see more than five nautical miles, two to five, or less than two. Very poor is under a kilometre.

The general synopsis is the big picture: where the highs and lows are now (with their pressure in hectopascals) and where they’re expected in 24 hours. These definitions are the Met Office’s, from its glossary. Distances in the synopsis use the atlas’s own scale bar, which says leagues, give or take.

02 Where the weather comes from

Elsewhere isn’t anywhere, but a forecast has to come from somewhere. So the whole chart above is laid over a real patch of ocean, 44° to 55° north and 49° to 15° west, stretched to fit: the real latitudes and longitudes are written round its edge. It’s open water all the way across, from about 150 nautical miles east of Newfoundland to the western edge of the real Shipping Forecast’s areas: Rockall and Shannon begin at 15°W, where Hereafter and Presently stop. Weather here mostly travels east, so what crosses Elsewhere today is usually on its way to Ireland and Britain.

Every area’s forecast is the real forecast for four or six points of water inside it, over the next 24 hours.

03 How it’s made

At 0000, 0600, 1200 and 1800 UTC, a small program on this site asks Open-Meteo for the forecast at those points: wind, gusts, weather, visibility, wave height and pressure, hour by hour. Open-Meteo passes on the output of national weather services’ computer models. The program then writes the bulletin the way the real one is written, by rules: Beaufort forces from wind speeds, sea states from wave heights, “veering” when the wind turns clockwise, a gale warning when the wind or its gusts reach gale force. Nobody checks it by hand. I wrote the rules and the areas’ names. The weather itself is real.

The voice is your own device’s. Browsers can read text aloud, and this page asks yours to, in a British English voice if it has one. The bell is made up on the spot from a few sine waves. It strikes the time in ship’s bells: one to eight strokes, one for each half hour of a four-hour watch, in pairs.

Not for navigation. This is a toy made out of a real forecast. If you’re going to sea, in these waters or any others, use the official forecasts for them.

Nothing about you is collected or kept. Your browser asks this site for the bulletin, and this site asks Open-Meteo for the weather, for everyone at once. Weather data by Open-Meteo.com, under CC BY 4.0.
